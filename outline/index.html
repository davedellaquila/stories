Story Ideas for the Area

Every idea on this list gets developed independently, the same way as the Kit Carson story — researched, written as its own page, and added to the collection. Dave's three picks lead the list.

— Dave's Picks

The three Dave named first, on Nov 15, 2026.

The Mormon Emigrant Trail Dave's pick

In 1848, Mormon Battalion veterans — fresh from the gold discovery their comrades helped trigger at Sutter's Mill — cut a wagon road east over the Sierra along today's Iron Mountain Road and Highway 88 corridor, heading home to their families in Utah. The road they blazed opened the Carson route to the emigration that followed.

Local tie · Iron Mountain Rd / Hwy 88 corridor

Sutter's Fort Dave's pick

John Sutter's New Helvetia: the Swiss immigrant's adobe empire at the edge of Mexican California, the fort everything and everyone ran through — Frémont, the emigrants, the gold seekers. And the gold found at his sawmill in Coloma that ruined him and built Sacramento.

Local tie · The fort the Kit Carson story ends at

José María Amador Dave's pick

The man this county is named for: a Californio soldier and majordomo of Mission San José, rancher in the East Bay valley that also bears his name, who came to the southern mines and dug gold in the diggings of his own namesake county.

Local tie · Amador County's namesake

— The Road Over the Mountains

Carson Pass country — the road itself is the main character of this whole collection.

The Carson Emigrant Road

1849–1852: thousands of wagons climbed the road the Mormons cut — Devil's Ladder, the grades above Caples Lake, the diaries of people who buried their oxen and kept walking. "Seeing the elephant" on the Carson route.

Local tie · Carson Pass, Hwy 88

Tragedy Spring

Three Mormon Battalion men — Daniel Browett, Ezra Allen, Henderson Cox — murdered at a spring on the trail in the summer of 1848. Their comrades found them, buried them, and named the place for what happened there. The name is still on the map.

Local tie · Hwy 88, near Silver Lake

Cook's Station Dave flagged it

Its own story, not a footnote: a working roadhouse on Highway 88 that began as a stage and teamster stop on the old Carson road in the 1860s and never really stopped serving travelers — emigrants, freighters, stage passengers, skiers, bikers, and everyone Dave knows who's ever pulled in for coffee at 5,000 feet.

Local tie · Still open on Hwy 88, Eldorado National Forest

Ham's Station Dave flagged it

Its own story too: the little station near the top of the grade that served the Carson road's wagons and stages and still anchors the highway — the name Caltrans reaches for when the chain controls go up ("four miles east of Ham's Station"), and a snow-country outpost with a history all its own.

Local tie · Hwy 88, above Pioneer

The Other Stations of the Carson Road

Kirkwood's, Caples Lake, and the toll stops that didn't survive: the innkeepers and winter keepers who fed, watered, and sometimes rescued everyone crossing the pass — the roadside world of the Sierra crossing, beyond the two stations still standing.

Local tie · Kirkwood & Caples Lake, Hwy 88

Hank Monk, the Stage Driver

The Sierra's most famous reinsman, who drove Horace Greeley over the mountains on the Placerville–Carson run and answered the worried editor with "Keep your seat, Horace, I'll get you there on time" — a line Lincoln loved to quote.

Local tie · The Placerville–Carson Valley stage run

The Pony Express over the Sierra

1860–61: riders out of Placerville climbing Johnson Pass in blizzards, changing horses at Yank's and Strawberry, carrying the mail that tied California to the Union on the eve of the Civil War. The Carson route's faster neighbor — an era companion piece.

Local tie · Placerville gateway, Hwy 50 corridor

The Donner Party Dave flagged it

The northern route's catastrophe, 1846–47: the Hastings Cutoff, the snow at Truckee Lake, the Forlorn Hope, and the relief parties that rode out from Sutter's Fort. Every Sierra crossing story in this collection — Carson's triumph included — is measured against this one.

Local tie · Donner Lake / Truckee route · Relief ran from Sutter's Fort

The Bear Flag Revolt Dave flagged it

June 1846: a band of American settlers rides into Sonoma, arrests General Vallejo in his own home, and declares a California Republic under a homemade flag — a grizzly, a star, a stripe. Twenty-five days later the U.S. flag replaces it. Frémont's ambiguous role, Carson's ride, and the Californio view of the strangest fortnight in California history.

Local tie · Sonoma · Ties to the Frémont–Carson story and José María Amador's account

The Mexican-American War in California Dave flagged it

How the war of 1846–48 reached the Pacific: the Bear Flag summer, Stockton and Frémont, the fight for Los Angeles, San Pasqual, the Treaty of Cahuenga signed at a ranch table, and Guadalupe Hidalgo handing California to the United States — told from the ground here, Californio side included.

Local tie · California theater of the war · Sets up everything that follows

Kearny's Army of the West Dave flagged it

General Stephen W. Kearny (spelled without the second "e") marches 1,700 men from Fort Leavenworth, takes Santa Fe without a shot, then crosses the desert with Kit Carson guiding — straight into San Pasqual, the bloodiest battle fought on California soil, and a rescue that nearly didn't come.

Local tie · San Pasqual · Carson connection to the Kit Carson story

— People of the Pass

The Kit Carson Story Published

Carson, Frémont, and the February 1844 winter crossing that gave Carson Pass its name. The collection's first story — already live.

Local tie · Carson Pass · Read it

The Snowshoe Thompson Story Published

Twenty winters carrying the U.S. mail on ten-foot skis between Placerville and Genoa — the Norwegian immigrant who was the Sierra's winter lifeline, and died still waiting for the government to pay him.

Local tie · The Carson route, on skis · Read it

Frémont on His Own

The Pathfinder beyond Carson: the Bear Flag summer, the court-martial, the first Republican run for the presidency, the Mariposa gold estate. A companion volume to the Kit Carson story, told from the other man's side of the partnership.

Local tie · Companion to the Kit Carson story

Black Bart, the Gentleman Bandit

Charles Boles robbed Mother Lode stages for eight years, politely, and twice left poems at the scene. His first and last robberies were in neighboring Calaveras County — and a dropped handkerchief with a laundry mark undid him.

Local tie · Funk Hill, Calaveras County

Joaquin Murrieta — Legend and Record

The Gold Rush's most famous Californio outlaw: what the record actually holds, what the legend added, and why California needed him. Hunted through the southern mines, displayed in a jar in San Francisco — a story about myth-making as much as banditry.

Local tie · The southern mines, Calaveras country

— Gold!

The Day It Started — Marshall at Coloma

January 24, 1848: James Marshall picks gold flakes out of the tailrace of Sutter's sawmill. The secret that couldn't be kept, the rush that swallowed the mill, the fort, and the discoverer — Marshall died poor in Kelsey, pointing at empty hills.

Local tie · Coloma, South Fork American River

The Mother Lode

The 120-mile vein system itself: geology as destiny. Why the gold ran in one long line down the foothills, and why the towns — Jackson, Sutter Creek, Angels Camp, Placerville — strung along it like beads on a string.

Local tie · The whole Hwy 49 corridor

Hangtown

Placerville's rough first years as Dry Diggings: the hanging tree, vigilante justice, and the Hangtown Fry — the oyster-and-bacon omelet a lucky miner invented to spend his gold on the most expensive breakfast in California.

Local tie · Placerville

Drytown

Amador County's first diggings, 1848: Dry Creek's gold made it boom to thousands almost overnight — and when the easy gold went, it faded into the quietest town in the county. The whole Gold Rush in one small town's arc.

Local tie · Drytown, Hwy 49

The County Seat War

Jackson vs. Mokelumne Hill, 1851–52: two rival towns, hot words, and a ballot box stolen in the night. How Jackson won the seat — and kept it when Amador County was carved out in 1854.

Local tie · Jackson & Mokelumne Hill

— The Mines

The Kennedy and the Argonaut

Jackson's two great mines: the Kennedy's iconic headframe and giant tailing wheels, the Argonaut next door — and August 1922, when fire trapped 47 men underground in California's worst hard-rock mining disaster, and the whole town dug for days to reach them.

Local tie · Jackson, visible from Hwy 49

Knight Foundry

Sutter Creek's 1873 foundry and machine shop — water-powered, line-shaft driven, the last of its kind in America, which cast and machined for the mines for over a century and still turns today.

Local tie · Sutter Creek · Already a Gazette favorite

Leland Stanford's Lincoln Mine

Before the railroad, the governorship, and the university: Stanford's Sacramento store money bought into Sutter Creek's Lincoln Mine, and Amador gold helped stake an empire.

Local tie · Sutter Creek

The Debris Wars

Hydraulic monitors washed whole hillsides into the rivers until the valley farms drowned in silt — and Judge Sawyer's 1884 decision ended the hydraulic era. Jackson's tailing wheels are the local monument to the fight over mine waste.

Local tie · The Kennedy tailing wheels, Jackson

— The First People

The roads in this collection were old before they were "discovered."

The Washoe and Miwok Roads

The trade routes over the pass that were ancient when Frémont arrived: Washoe summer country in Hope Valley, Da'aw — the lake — Miwok trails between the valley and the mountains, and what the crossing looked like before it had a name in English.

Local tie · Hope Valley, Carson Pass, Lake Tahoe

Chaw'se — The Grinding Rock

At Pine Grove, a limestone outcrop holds the largest collection of bedrock mortar cups in North America — generations of Miwok women grinding acorns in one place. The roundhouse, the park, and the living culture it anchors.

Local tie · Indian Grinding Rock SHP, Pine Grove

The Jackson Rancheria

The Miwuk story into the present: a small, land-poor rancheria's long road through the twentieth century to the modern era — told history-first, with care.

Local tie · Jackson Rancheria, Amador County

— Immigrants Who Stayed

The Great Immigration Dave flagged it

The overview story Dave asked not to lose: the great influx of Italian immigrants — and the other Europeans who came with them, Serbs, Cornish, Croats, Germans, French, Basques — into what's now Amador County. They came for the mines and stayed for the land, and they remade the county's food, faith, vineyards, and surnames. The chapters below tell it group by group; this one tells it whole.

Local tie · The whole county · Overview for the stories below

Chew Kee of Fiddletown

The Chinese herbal doctor whose rammed-earth store still stands on Fiddletown's Main Street — and the Chinese community of miners, merchants, and gardeners who built a quarter of the town and were written out of most of its histories.

Local tie · Chew Kee Store, Fiddletown

St. Sava and the Serbs of Jackson

Serbian miners built St. Sava in Jackson in 1894 — the first Serbian Orthodox church in America. Serbian Christmas on January 7, the cemetery on the hill, and the community that kept both for 130 years.

Local tie · Jackson

The Italians of the Mother Lode

Italian stonemasons, gardeners, and winemakers put down the deepest roots of any immigrant group in Amador County — Sutter Creek's Italian picnic tradition reaches back to the 1880s, and their stone walls and vineyards are still on the land.

Local tie · Sutter Creek & the Shenandoah Valley

The Cornish — Cousin Jacks of the Deep Mines

Hard-rock mining was a Cornish trade, and the Kennedy and Argonaut ran on Cornish skill: the captains, the timbermen, the pasty in the lunch pail. How Cornwall's miners crossed an ocean to become the backbone of Amador's deep mines — part of the same European influx.

Local tie · Jackson's deep mines

— Towns with Stories

The Town That Lost Its Name

Fiddletown's fiddlers were real — Missouri miners who fiddled away the dry winter of 1849. But in 1878 the state legislature decided "Fiddletown" was undignified and renamed it Oleta. It took the town 54 years to get its name back, in 1932.

Local tie · Fiddletown itself

Volcano and Old Abe

The Gold Rush town in its bowl of hills: a near-miss Civil War, a brass cannon named Old Abe smuggled in to keep the peace, limestone caves, and the little theatre company that refused to let the town go quiet.

Local tie · Volcano

Preston Castle

The Romanesque castle on Ione's hill is a reform school: the Preston School of Industry, 1894, where California sent its wards to learn trades. The boys, the bricks they made, the escapes, and the long fight to save the building.

Local tie · Ione

Amador City

Among California's smallest incorporated cities, and nearly a ghost town more than once — the Keystone Mine's whistle, the false-front main street, and the town that keeps declining to disappear.

Local tie · Amador City, Hwy 49

Murphys and the Murphy Brothers

Daniel Murphy came over the Sierra with the first wagon train in 1844; four years later he and his brother John found some of the richest diggings in the southern mines, and their camp became Murphys — "Queen of the Sierra."

Local tie · Murphys, Calaveras County

Mark Twain's Frog

Angels Camp, winter 1865: an unknown Sam Clemens hears a tall tale in a bar and writes "The Celebrated Jumping Frog of Calaveras County." It made Mark Twain — and the frog jump still runs every spring at the county fair.

Local tie · Angels Camp, Calaveras County

Columbia, Gem of the Southern Mines

The best-preserved Gold Rush town in California survived because the state made it a park instead of letting it burn or fade — brick buildings, a working stagecoach, and the rush preserved in amber. A wider-Mother-Lode excursion.

Local tie · Columbia SHP, Tuolumne County

— Land, Water, and Fire

The Mokelumne

Amador's river: Miwok salmon water, Gold Rush ditch water, Electra's powerhouses, and EBMUD's Pardee Dam — a century of fights over who the river belongs to, running right past the county's western edge.

Local tie · The Mokelumne River & Pardee

The Cosumnes — The River with No Dams

The last free-flowing river on the Sierra's west slope: no major dam from source to delta, salmon still running in wet years, and the preserve that protects its valley oaks.

Local tie · Cosumnes River, Sacramento/Amador edge

The Big Trees

1852: a hunter chasing a grizzly walks into a grove of trees wider than houses. The discovery of the giant sequoias, the felled "Discovery Tree" that shocked the world, and the long argument that helped invent conservation.

Local tie · Calaveras Big Trees, Hwy 4

The Eldorado

The national forest Dave is driving road by road: how the Eldorado was stitched together from forest reserves, its lookouts and logging roads, the Desolation Wilderness — and what a thousand miles of forest road actually records.

Local tie · Eldorado National Forest · Dave's roads project

The Rubicon

Georgetown to Lake Tahoe over the roughest famous road in the Eldorado — from Native trail to emigrant-era track to the most storied 4x4 route in America.

Local tie · Rubicon Trail, Eldorado NF

Daffodil Hill

Every spring a hillside near Volcano erupts in daffodils — planted bulb by bulb by the McLaughlin family since Gold Rush days, in memory of their daughter, until the hill itself became the memorial.

Local tie · Near Volcano, Amador County

The Caldor Fire

August 2021: a fire starts near Pollock Pines and runs for two months — Grizzly Flats gone in a night, the Hwy 88 corridor braced, Tahoe evacuated. The fire that changed how this country thinks about summer. A modern chapter, told close to home.

Local tie · El Dorado / Amador, 2021

Vaira Ranch Dave flagged it

A working cattle ranch on the oak savanna west of Ione that quietly became one of the most important pieces of ground in climate science. Since 2000, a UC Berkeley research tower on the Vaira family's land has measured the grassland breathing — carbon in, carbon out, season by season — building one of the longest records of its kind on Earth. Fran Vaira's grazing and brush work, blue oaks over annual grasses, vernal pools in a wet spring: the foothills as a living laboratory. And a coda close to home: the ranch's name is so often misspelled "Varia" that even the mapping services get it wrong — the Gazette corrected it in print the day Dave added this story to the list.

Local tie · Vaira Ranch Rd & Hwy 49, Ione / Drytown

The Research Station Dave flagged it

Standing in the grass at Vaira Ranch is a scaffold tower hung with instruments, listening to the land breathe. It is AmeriFlux site US-Var, run by UC Berkeley's biometeorology lab since 2000 — one of the longest-running grassland flux stations on Earth. Day and night, season after season, it records how much carbon the savanna takes up in a wet year and how much it gives back in a drought, and its numbers flow into the global FLUXNET record that climate models are tested against. World-class science, quietly ticking away in a pasture most drivers pass without a glance.

Local tie · Vaira Ranch, near Ione

— Wine Country

The Oldest Vines Dave flagged it

Dave's point, and he's right to make it: the oldest vineyards in California are here, in Amador County. The Shenandoah Valley's Grandpère Vineyard was planted in 1869 — the oldest documented Zinfandel vines in America, still producing. Gold Rush wine, Prohibition's near-kill, and the families like the Deavers who kept the old vines alive.

Local tie · Shenandoah Valley, Plymouth

The Foothill Wine Revival

The 1970s: Cary Gott founds Montevina, outsiders and old families bet on Barbera and Zinfandel at altitude, and Amador goes from forgotten diggings to one of California's most distinctive wine countries — the valley Dave and Nan's Cooper Vineyards calls home.

Local tie · Shenandoah Valley · Cooper Vineyards country

— The Other Passes

A driving series for the passes Dave runs — Carson was only the first.

Ebbetts Pass

John Ebbetts claimed a crossing in 1851; the road over Hwy 4's Pacific Grade became the emigrant and supply route to the Calaveras Big Trees and the eastern Sierra — and the skinniest, steepest state highway in the Sierra.

Local tie · Hwy 4 · Dave's five-pass country

Sonora Pass

The Sonora Road of 1852, cut by Sonora merchants to pull emigrant trade to their diggings — today Hwy 108, the second-highest paved pass in the Sierra, with grades that still make drivers pay attention.

Local tie · Hwy 108 · Dave's five-pass country

Monitor and Luther — The Quiet Passes

The smaller crossings that complete the set: Monitor Pass, opened as a toll road to the Monitor mining district, and Luther Pass, the quiet link between Hope Valley and Tahoe that Snowshoe Thompson's successors used. The passes nobody writes about — which is the point.

Local tie · Hwys 89/395 · Dave's five-pass country
